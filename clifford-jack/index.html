Imaging and fluid biomarkers for the biological definition, staging and prognosis of Alzheimer's disease.

  • MRI hippocampal volumetry. Showed that MRI hippocampal volume distinguishes Alzheimer's disease from normal aging (Neurology 1992) and predicts progression from mild cognitive impairment (Neurology 1999).
  • Biomarker cascade model. Proposed a hypothetical model of the temporal ordering of Alzheimer's biomarkers (Lancet Neurology 2010), later updated (Lancet Neurology 2013).
  • Biological definition. Proposed the A/T/N biomarker classification and led the NIA-AA research framework (Alzheimer's & Dementia 2018) and the revised Alzheimer's Association diagnostic and staging criteria (Alzheimer's & Dementia 2024).
  • Population studies. In the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging, characterized age- and sex-specific prevalence of amyloidosis, tauopathy and neurodegeneration (Lancet Neurology 2017) and absolute risk of cognitive impairment by amyloid PET level (Lancet Neurology 2025).

  1. 1
    Biological ADAlzheimer's defined by brain changes, not symptoms

    The 2018 NIA-AA research framework defines Alzheimer's disease in living people by its underlying brain changes, measured with biomarkers of amyloid, tau and neurodegeneration (AT(N)), rather than by symptoms. Cognitive stage is described separately.

    Jack et al. 2018 · Alzheimers DementConsensus research framework of the NIA-AA workgroup
  2. 2
    Core 1one abnormal amyloid PET, CSF or accurate blood test is enough for diagnosis

    The 2024 Alzheimer's Association criteria say Alzheimer's disease begins with brain changes while people have no symptoms. An abnormal early "Core 1" biomarker, such as amyloid PET, an approved spinal fluid test or an accurate blood test (especially p-tau217), is enough to diagnose it.

    Jack et al. 2024 · Alzheimers DementRevised diagnostic and staging criteria of the Alzheimer's Association workgroup
  3. 3
    Amyloid firstthen neurodegeneration, which tracks symptoms

    A model of Alzheimer's biomarkers proposed that amyloid markers (low CSF amyloid-beta 42, raised amyloid PET) become abnormal first while people are still cognitively normal, and markers of neuronal injury (CSF tau, MRI atrophy, low FDG-PET) become abnormal later and track how severe symptoms are.

    Jack et al. 2010 · Lancet NeurolHypothetical model built from published biomarker evidence
  4. 4
    A/T/Namyloid, tau and neurodegeneration, each rated positive or negative

    The A/T/N scheme sorts 7 major Alzheimer's biomarkers into 3 groups, amyloid (A), tau (T) and neurodegeneration (N), each rated normal or abnormal, giving profiles such as A+/T+/N-. It describes what is in the brain without assuming an order of events or a diagnosis.

    Jack et al. 2016 · NeurologyProposed classification scheme
  5. 5
    ~15 yearsfor brain amyloid to rise from moderate to high

    In 260 people with repeated amyloid PET scans, amyloid built up slowly at low levels, fastest at mid levels, and then levelled off. Moving from an SUVR of 1.5 to 2.5 took about 15 years, a long window for prevention treatment.

    Jack et al. 2013 · NeurologySerial amyloid PET in 260 adults aged 70-92 (Mayo Clinic Study of Aging and ADRC)
  6. 6
    Updated modelamyloid may speed up tau that started earlier

    The 2013 update to the biomarker model indexed people by time rather than symptom severity, allowed for differences between people in how much decline the same pathology causes, and proposed that amyloid and tau may start independently, with amyloid accelerating tau already present in limbic and brainstem areas.

    Jack et al. 2013 · Lancet NeurolUpdated hypothetical model reviewing evidence since 2010
  7. 7
    84% vs 69%lifetime risk of MCI at centiloid 100 vs 5 (women with APOE4, from age 75)

    In 5,158 cognitively unimpaired adults, lifetime risk of mild cognitive impairment and dementia rose steadily with amyloid PET level. For women carrying APOE4 starting at age 75, lifetime risk of MCI was 69% at centiloid 5 and 84% at centiloid 100; for men carrying APOE4 it was 56% and 77%.

    Jack et al. 2025 · Lancet NeurolPopulation-based cohort, 5,158 cognitively unimpaired and 700 MCI participants, multistate modelling
  8. 8
    >90%of people aged 85 have at least one abnormal brain biomarker

    In cognitively unimpaired people aged 50 to 95, having all-normal amyloid, tau and neurodegeneration markers was most common until the late 70s, after about 80 having all three abnormal was most common, and by 85 more than 90% of men and women had at least one abnormality.

    Jack et al. 2017 · Lancet NeurolCross-sectional population study, 435 with amyloid PET, tau PET and MRI plus 1,113 with amyloid PET and MRI
  9. 9
    85%of Alzheimer's patients below the healthy range of hippocampal volume

    An MRI method for measuring the hippocampus separated 20 people with Alzheimer's dementia from 22 healthy older controls, with 85% of patients falling below the range of the controls, across the whole age range studied.

    Jack et al. 1992 · NeurologyCase-control MRI volumetry, 20 patients and 22 controls
  10. 10
    27 of 80people with MCI developed Alzheimer's; smaller hippocampus predicted who

    In 80 people with mild cognitive impairment followed for about 33 months, 27 developed dementia. A smaller hippocampus on MRI at the start predicted conversion to Alzheimer's, even after accounting for age, memory tests, APOE genotype and heart risk factors.

    Jack et al. 1999 · NeurologyProspective cohort, 80 patients with MCI
  11. 11
    3.7% vs 1.7%yearly hippocampal loss in MCI decliners vs stable controls

    In 129 older adults scanned twice about 3 years apart, the hippocampus shrank faster in people whose memory got worse. Yearly loss was 1.7% in stable controls, 2.8% in controls who declined, 2.6% in stable MCI, 3.7% in MCI who declined, and 3.5% in Alzheimer's.

    Jack et al. 2000 · NeurologyLongitudinal MRI cohort, 129 participants
  12. 12
    Atrophy, not amyloidtracks memory decline over a year

    In 61 people with repeat amyloid PET and MRI one year apart, amyloid rose slowly at the same rate whatever their clinical stage and did not track cognitive change, while brain shrinkage was faster with worse stage and tracked worsening on memory and function scores.

    Jack et al. 2009 · BrainSerial PIB PET and MRI, 21 normal, 32 MCI and 8 Alzheimer's
  13. 13
    89%of people with Alzheimer's dementia had both high amyloid and high tau

    Among 1,343 people with amyloid and tau PET, abnormal tau rarely appeared without abnormal amyloid, but abnormal amyloid without tau was common. High amyloid was compatible with normal thinking, but highly abnormal tau was not, and 89% of people with Alzheimer's clinical syndrome were high on both.

    Jack et al. 2019 · BrainAmyloid and tau PET in 1,343 participants across 5 clinical groups
  14. 14
    Centiloid 19cut point for an abnormal amyloid PET scan

    Comparing five ways of setting cut points, the group chose an amyloid PET threshold of SUVR 1.42 (centiloid 19) based on reliable worsening over time, and set lenient and conservative cut points for tau PET, FDG-PET and MRI cortical thickness.

    Jack et al. 2017 · Alzheimers DementMethods comparison in Mayo Clinic imaging cohorts
  15. 15
    42%of cognitively normal 89-year-olds had both amyloid and neurodegeneration

    In 985 cognitively normal people aged 50 to 89, all had normal amyloid and neurodegeneration markers at 50, but only 17% did by 89, when 42% had both abnormal. Amyloid without neurodegeneration peaked at 28% around age 74 and was more frequent in APOE4 carriers.

    Jack et al. 2014 · Lancet NeurolCross-sectional population study, 985 participants
  16. 16
    11xhigher rate of new neurodegeneration at 85 than at 65

    In 4,049 people without dementia, rates of moving to a more abnormal biomarker state rose steeply with age. At 85 compared with 65, the move from normal to neurodegeneration alone was 11 times as frequent (17.2 vs 1.6 per 100 person-years), while new amyloid plateaued after about 75.

    Jack et al. 2016 · Lancet NeurolPopulation-based longitudinal cohort, 4,049 participants, multistate Markov model
  17. 17
    74% vs 62%lifetime dementia risk at 65 for women vs men with APOE4 and high amyloid

    In 4,984 people without dementia followed a median of 9.4 years, high amyloid doubled the hazard of dementia (HR 2.11). Among APOE4 carriers with high amyloid, remaining lifetime dementia risk from age 65 was 74% for women and 62% for men.

    Jack et al. 2022 · Brain CommunPopulation-based cohort, 4,984 participants, multistate models with competing mortality
  18. 18
    R² 0.26 → 0.31added by amyloid, tau and MRI to predicting memory decline

    In 480 older people without dementia followed for about 4.8 years, memory declined fastest when amyloid was abnormal together with tau or neurodegeneration. Adding the three scans to age, sex, education, health conditions and APOE improved prediction only modestly, from R² 0.26 to 0.31.

    Jack et al. 2019 · JAMAPopulation-based cohort, 480 participants with amyloid PET, tau PET and MRI
  19. 19
    C = 0.90plasma p-tau181 for intermediate/high Alzheimer's pathology in impaired people

    In 1,136 people with same-day amyloid PET, tau PET and blood draw, plasma biomarkers with risk factors predicted PET stages with acceptable to excellent accuracy. Plasma p-tau181 alone identified intermediate or high Alzheimer's pathology with C = 0.87 in unimpaired and 0.90 in impaired people.

    Jack et al. 2023 · BrainCross-sectional study, 1,136 participants (864 unimpaired, 272 impaired)
  20. 20
    Group, not personp-tau217 and amyloid PET predict average memory decline

    In 645 cognitively unimpaired people, plasma p-tau217 and amyloid PET, along with age, were the key independent predictors of memory decline. They predicted average decline in groups well, but prediction for any single person was wide.

    Jack et al. 2024 · Alzheimers DementPopulation-based cohort, 645 participants
  21. 21
    AUC 0.81-0.86for plasma p-tau to flag abnormal amyloid PET in the community

    In 1,329 community members aged 30 to 98, plasma p-tau181 and p-tau217 predicted abnormal amyloid PET (AUC 0.81 to 0.86). Chronic kidney disease raised p-tau about as much as brain amyloid did, so other illnesses must be considered when reading the tests.

    Mielke et al. 2022 · Nat MedPopulation-based study, 1,329 participants
  22. 22
    90%of positive amyloid scans matched intermediate/high pathology at autopsy

    Among 259 people with a positive amyloid PET scan who later had an autopsy, 90% had intermediate or high Alzheimer's neuropathologic change. Even 10 or more years after a positive scan, only 3 of 39 had low or no change.

    Burkett et al. 2026 · Alzheimers DementImaging-autopsy study, 259 participants
  23. 23
    0.5% vs 3%yearly tau PET increase in unimpaired vs impaired people with amyloid

    In 126 people with two tau PET scans, tau did not build up measurably in unimpaired people with normal amyloid. It rose about 0.5% a year in unimpaired people with abnormal amyloid and about 3% a year in impaired people, across the whole brain rather than one region at a time.

    Jack et al. 2018 · BrainSerial tau PET, 126 participants
  24. 24
    Age 57 vs 64when 10% of APOE4 carriers vs non-carriers are amyloid positive

    In 1,246 cognitively normal people aged 30 to 95, amyloid stayed low until about 70 and then rose. APOE4 did not change memory or hippocampal size, but 10% of carriers had positive amyloid scans by 57 versus 64 for non-carriers. Men had worse memory and smaller hippocampi than women.

    Jack et al. 2015 · JAMA NeurolCross-sectional study, 1,246 cognitively normal participants
  25. 25
    ~23%of clinically normal people over 65 have SNAP

    Suspected non-Alzheimer pathophysiology (SNAP), brain injury markers without amyloid, is found in about 23% of clinically normal people over 65 and 25% of people with mild impairment. It is less linked to APOE4, and people with it do worse than those with all-normal markers.

    Jack et al. 2016 · Nat Rev NeurolPerspective reviewing published cohort data
  26. 26
    8.7 yearslater onset of impairment with high vs low lifetime intellectual enrichment (APOE4 carriers)

    In 1,995 older adults without dementia, more education, work complexity and mid- to late-life mental activity went with better thinking. For APOE4 carriers, high lifetime intellectual enrichment was linked to cognitive impairment starting about 8.7 years later than low enrichment.

    Vemuri et al. 2014 · JAMA NeurolPopulation-based cohort, 1,995 participants
  27. 27
    -4%hippocampal volume with diabetes starting in midlife

    In 1,437 people without dementia, diabetes that began in midlife was linked to a 4% smaller hippocampus, 2.9% smaller brain, more small strokes and about double the odds of mild cognitive impairment. Midlife high blood pressure was linked to strokes and white matter damage.

    Roberts et al. 2014 · NeurologyPopulation-based cross-sectional study, 1,437 participants, median age 80
