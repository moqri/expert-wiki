Epigenetic clocks, explained from the evidence

Your DNA keeps time. Here is what it can tell you.

Epigenetic clocks read chemical marks on DNA to estimate age across most human tissues, and across mammals. Below, what the studies show is set out as 33 claims, one per line, each with a number, a short statement, and the single paper that supports it. Glossary
DNA methylation
A chemical tag (a methyl group) on DNA, mostly at CpG sites, that helps switch genes on or off. Its pattern changes with age in predictable ways.
CpG site
A place in the genome where a C is followed by a G. Clocks read the methylation level at a few hundred to a few thousand of these sites.
Epigenetic clock
A formula that turns methylation levels at chosen CpG sites into an age estimate or an aging rate.
Chronological age
Age in years since birth.
Biological age
An estimate of how old the body behaves. Each clock defines it differently, and there is no agreed gold standard.
Age acceleration
The gap between clock age and calendar age, after adjusting for calendar age. Positive means older than expected.
First-generation clock
Trained to predict calendar age, for example the Horvath and Hannum clocks.
Second-generation clock
Trained to predict health outcomes or death, for example PhenoAge and GrimAge.
Pace-of-aging clock
Estimates how fast someone is aging now, rather than how old they are, for example DunedinPACE.
Test-retest reliability
How closely two measurements of the same sample agree. Often reported as an intraclass correlation (ICC), where 1 is perfect agreement.
Principal-component (PC) clock
A clock rebuilt on summary patterns across many CpG sites, which makes it less sensitive to noise at any one site.
Methylation array
The lab chip most clocks are built on, such as Illumina's 450K and EPIC arrays. Results can shift between array versions and labs.
Hazard ratio
How much the risk of an event, such as death, changes per unit of a measure. 1.10 means 10% higher risk per unit.
Pluripotent stem cell
A cell that can become any cell type. Embryonic and reprogrammed (iPS) cells read close to age zero on the Horvath clock.
