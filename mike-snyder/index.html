Aging

Mike Snyder, PhD

Stanford University School of MedicineDepartment of Genetics

Tracks the same people for years with blood tests, wearables and many molecular measurements to see how health, disease and aging unfold in each individual.

Mike Snyder's work follows individuals over time rather than comparing groups at a single moment. Starting with a 14-month profile of Snyder's own genome, RNA, proteins, metabolites and antibodies, Snyder's group built a Stanford cohort of about 100 people, many at risk of type 2 diabetes, who gave samples every few months for up to eight years. These profiles turned up dozens of clinically actionable findings and showed that each person's healthy baseline is distinct.

Applied to aging, the same data show that people age along different molecular routes, called ageotypes (for example metabolic, immune, liver or kidney), and that many molecules do not change at a steady pace: large shifts cluster around age 44 and again around age 60. Lipids, the microbiome and DNA methylation all show age-linked changes, and people with insulin resistance tend to show faster or disturbed changes.

A second thread is continuous monitoring outside the clinic. Consumer smartwatches flagged most COVID-19 infections, often before symptoms, and wearable signals predicted some routine lab results. Continuous glucose monitors revealed that blood sugar spikes are common even in people with normal standard tests, and that the shape of a glucose curve can point to the underlying cause of prediabetes.

Earlier, Snyder's lab helped invent core genomics tools, including protein microarrays and RNA sequencing, and contributes to efforts to standardize biomarkers of aging so they can be used to test longevity interventions.

  1. 1
    44 and 60years of age when many molecules shift most sharply

    Molecular aging is not steady. Across many types of molecules, the biggest changes clustered around age 44 (cardiovascular, lipid and alcohol metabolism) and age 60 (immune regulation and carbohydrate metabolism).

    Shen et al. 2024 · Nat AgingLongitudinal multi-omics study, 108 adults aged 25 to 75, followed up to 6.8 years

    Ageotypesdifferent molecular routes of aging in different people

    People age along different molecular pathways, called ageotypes, and personal aging markers could be seen changing within just 2 to 3 years. Insulin-resistant and insulin-sensitive people showed distinct aging patterns.

    Ahadi et al. 2020 · Nat MedLongitudinal multi-omics study, 106 healthy adults aged 29 to 75

    Deep, repeated profiling of people at risk of type 2 diabetes produced more than 67 clinically actionable health discoveries, and most participants changed their diet and exercise as a result of taking part.

    Schüssler-Fiorenza Rose et al. 2019 · Nat MedProspective cohort, 109 people sampled quarterly for up to 8 years (median 2.8)

    ~90%of age-related DNA methylation gain falls in PRC2 target regions

    Regions bound by the PRC2 complex in stem cells gain DNA methylation with age in all dividing body cells examined, accounting for about 90% of age-related methylation gain. Their average, the PRC2-AgeIndex, is proposed as a universal aging biomarker that can compare anti-aging interventions.

    Moqri et al. 2024 · Nat CommunGenome-wide DNA methylation analysis across somatic tissues
