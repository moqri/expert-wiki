AgingAlzheimer's

Bruce A. Yankner, MD, PhD

Harvard Medical SchoolDepartment of Genetics

Studies why some aging brains stay sharp while others develop Alzheimer's disease, from the toxicity of amyloid to the protective protein REST and the role of lithium in the brain.

Yankner's early work helped establish that amyloid-β, the protein that builds up in Alzheimer's plaques, can itself kill neurons. Yankner's group showed that a fragment of the amyloid precursor protein was toxic to neurons, that toxicity depends on amyloid-β forming fibrils, that those fibrils trigger the tau changes seen in tangles, and that plaque-level amyloid-β kills neurons in the brains of old monkeys but not young ones, suggesting that aging itself makes the brain vulnerable.

Yankner's group then studied normal brain aging in people. Gene activity in the human frontal cortex from age 26 to 106 showed that genes for learning, memory and energy production begin to fall after about age 40, alongside rising DNA damage at those genes. Comparing humans, monkeys and mice showed that this shutdown of neuron genes is far stronger in primates.

A central finding is the protective role of REST, a protein that switches off genes. REST rises in the neurons of healthy older people but is lost in mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's disease, and higher REST tracks with keeping memory and living longer. In mice and worms, REST calms excess neural activity and extends lifespan, and it acts as a checkpoint that slows the onset of Alzheimer's changes. Stem cell models from people with Alzheimer's or APOE4 show REST failing early.

Most recently, Yankner's group found that natural lithium in the brain falls in mild cognitive impairment, is trapped by amyloid plaques in Alzheimer's disease, and that lithium loss speeds Alzheimer's changes in mice, while low-dose lithium orotate prevented them. These are animal results; whether lithium replacement helps people still needs clinical trials.

  1. 1
    ~50% less lithiumin the brain speeds up Alzheimer's changes and memory loss in mice

    Of the metals measured, only lithium was lower in the brains of people with mild cognitive impairment, and in Alzheimer's disease it was further trapped by amyloid plaques. Halving brain lithium in mice increased amyloid plaques, tau buildup, brain inflammation and loss of synapses, and sped memory decline. Low-dose lithium orotate, a form that is not trapped by amyloid, prevented these changes and memory loss in Alzheimer's mice and old normal mice; human trials are still needed.

    Aron et al. 2025 · NatureHuman brain metal measurements plus mouse diet and treatment studies
  2. 2
    RESTrises in healthy aging neurons and is lost in Alzheimer's disease

    The gene-silencing protein REST is switched on in neurons of the cortex and hippocampus during normal aging, where it turns off genes that promote cell death and Alzheimer's pathology and turns on stress defenses. REST is lost in mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's disease, and REST levels in aging tracked closely with keeping cognitive function and with longevity. Mice lacking REST in the brain developed age-related neurodegeneration.

    Lu et al. 2014 · NaturePostmortem human brains, mouse knockouts, cultured neurons and worms
  3. 3
    After age 40genes for memory and energy in the human cortex start to decline

    Gene activity in the frontal cortex of people aged 26 to 106 showed a set of genes for synaptic plasticity, vesicle transport and mitochondria that drop after age 40, followed by a rise in stress response and DNA repair genes. DNA damage was markedly higher at the promoters of the declining genes, suggesting brain aging begins early in adult life.

    Lu et al. 2004 · NatureGene expression profiling of human frontal cortex across ages 26–106, plus cultured human neurons
  4. 4
    Lower neural excitationin the cortex of people who lived longest

    People with extended longevity had a cortex gene signature with lower activity of genes for neural excitation and synapses, and higher REST. In worms, reducing neural excitation extended lifespan, and adding the worm version of REST suppressed excitation and lengthened life. REST-deficient mice had overactive cortex during aging.

    Zullo et al. 2019 · NatureHuman brain transcriptomes from long-lived people, plus worm and mouse genetics
  5. 5
    CheckpointREST holds back Alzheimer's changes in older people without symptoms

    Many older people carry Alzheimer's pathology without cognitive decline. REST was induced in cognitively intact older people and in Alzheimer's mice at the onset of amyloid and tau buildup, lowering gamma secretase, tau kinases and cell death genes. Deleting REST in two Alzheimer's mouse models sped amyloid and tau buildup, neurodegeneration and memory loss, while adding REST in the hippocampus reduced them.

    Aron et al. 2023 · Nat CommunPostmortem human brains and two Alzheimer's mouse models with REST deleted or added
  6. 6
    Old brains onlyplaque-level amyloid-β killed neurons in aged but not young monkeys

    Injecting fibrillar amyloid-β at plaque-like concentrations into the cortex of aged rhesus monkeys caused severe neuron loss, tau phosphorylation and microglial growth, but not in young adult monkeys. Toxicity was weaker in aged marmosets and not significant in aged rats, suggesting long-lived primates are uniquely vulnerable.

    Geula et al. 1998 · Nat MedBrain microinjection study in rhesus monkeys, marmosets and rats
  7. 7
    APOE4changes neurons, astrocytes and microglia in human stem cell models

    Gene-edited human stem cell brain cells showed that APOE4 changes hundreds of genes in each cell type. APOE4 neurons made more Aβ42, APOE4 astrocytes took up less amyloid-β and stored excess cholesterol, and APOE4 microglia-like cells cleared less amyloid-β. Converting APOE4 to APOE3 in cells from people with Alzheimer's reduced several Alzheimer's-related changes.

    Lin et al. 2018 · NeuronCRISPR-edited isogenic iPSC-derived neurons, astrocytes and microglia-like cells
  8. 8
    Fibrils, not solubleamyloid-β triggers tau changes like those in Alzheimer's tangles

    Fibrillar amyloid-β, but not soluble or amorphous forms, caused tau phosphorylation at Alzheimer's-linked sites in rat and human neurons. The modified tau detached from microtubules and collected in the cell body, as in the Alzheimer's brain, linking amyloid to tangle formation.

    Busciglio et al. 1995 · NeuronPrimary rat hippocampal and human cortical neuron cultures
  9. 9
    Neurotoxica fragment of the amyloid precursor protein kills neurons

    Cells engineered to make an amyloid-containing fragment of the amyloid precursor protein degenerated as they became neuron-like, and their medium was toxic to hippocampal neurons. Removing the amyloid peptide with an antibody removed the toxicity, an early sign that amyloid may cause, not just mark, Alzheimer's damage.

    Yankner et al. 1989 · ScienceTransfected cell lines and primary hippocampal neuron cultures
  10. 10
    Early REST failurein neurons grown from people with Alzheimer's or APOE4

    Neural cells made from stem cells of people with sporadic Alzheimer's, and from APOE4 gene-edited cells, matured too early and renewed less. REST entered the nucleus and bound DNA less, and the nuclear lamina was disrupted, suggesting gene network changes may start the Alzheimer's cascade.

    Meyer et al. 2019 · Cell RepiPSC-derived neural progenitors, neurons and cerebral organoids
  11. 11
    Requires fibrilsamyloid-β toxicity, which the dye Congo red blocks

    Fibrillar amyloid-β, like compact plaques, killed hippocampal neurons and reduced synapses, while amorphous aggregates like diffuse plaques were harmless. Congo red, a dye that binds amyloid fibrils, blocked the toxicity.

    Lorenzo 1994 · Proc Natl Acad Sci U S APrimary rat hippocampal neuron cultures
  12. 12
    Shared aging changesand a primate-specific shutdown of neuron genes

    Comparing the aging cortex of humans, rhesus monkeys and mice, a few changes were shared by all three, such as more APOD and less CAMK4. Humans and monkeys showed far stronger age-related repression of neuron and synapse genes, including inhibitory GABA genes, which was not explained by loss of neurons or synapses.

    Loerch et al. 2008 · PLoS OneGenome-wide cortex gene expression in humans, rhesus macaques and mice
  13. 13
    3–4×more reactive oxygen species in Down syndrome neurons before they die

    Neurons from fetal Down syndrome brain developed normally in culture but then degenerated by apoptosis, after showing a three- to fourfold rise in reactive oxygen species. Antioxidants prevented the degeneration, pointing to a defect that may predispose people with Down syndrome to Alzheimer's.

    Busciglio 1995 · NatureCultured cortical neurons from fetal Down syndrome and control brain
  14. 14
    SIRT1 movesto repair DNA breaks, shifting gene activity as in the aging brain

    The longevity protein SIRT1 normally silences repetitive DNA and many genes. After DNA damage it leaves these sites to help repair breaks, causing gene changes that resemble the aging mouse brain. More SIRT1 improved survival in mice with genomic instability and suppressed age-related gene changes.

    Oberdoerffer et al. 2008 · CellMouse embryonic stem cells and mouse models
  15. 15
    Presenilin-1 mutationsdestabilize β-catenin and make neurons more likely to die

    Presenilin-1, the gene most often mutated in early-onset inherited Alzheimer's, binds and stabilizes β-catenin. Disease mutations reduced this, β-catenin was markedly lower in brains of patients with presenilin-1 mutations, and loss of β-catenin signaling made neurons more vulnerable to amyloid-β.

    Zhang et al. 1998 · NatureTransgenic mice, patient brains and cultured neurons
  16. 16
    Caspase-12mediates amyloid-β killing of neurons through stress in the endoplasmic reticulum

    Caspase-12 sits in the endoplasmic reticulum and is activated by stress there. Mice lacking caspase-12 resisted this kind of cell death, and their cortical neurons were protected from amyloid-β but not from other death signals.

    Nakagawa et al. 2000 · NatureCaspase-12 knockout mice and cultured cortical neurons
  17. 17
    Autophagyfalls in the normally aging brain but rises in Alzheimer's

    A genome-wide screen showed that reactive oxygen species switch on autophagy, the cell's cleanup system, including in response to amyloid-β. Autophagy genes were turned down during normal human brain aging but turned up in Alzheimer's brains, possibly as compensation.

    Lipinski et al. 2010 · Proc Natl Acad Sci U S AGenome-wide screen plus human brain gene expression
  18. 18
    Shorter lifefrom a brain inflammation pathway that damages inhibitory neurons

    Raising neuronal C/EBPβ, which is switched on by amyloid-β and inflammation, shortened lifespan in mice in a dose-dependent way by killing inhibitory GABA neurons and overexciting the brain. The same pathway shortened life in worms, and its components were lower in long-lived humans and inversely related to REST.

    Xia et al. 2022 · Sci AdvTransgenic mice, worms and human brain tissue
  19. 19
    Amylin fibrilskill insulin-making cells, as amyloid-β kills neurons

    Fibrils of human amylin, the main component of pancreatic amyloid in type 2 diabetes, were toxic to insulin-producing beta cells from rats and humans by triggering apoptosis, pointing to a shared amyloid mechanism in Alzheimer's and type 2 diabetes.

    Lorenzo et al. 1994 · NatureCultured rat and human pancreatic islet cells
  20. 20
    Dopamine-dependentα-synuclein toxicity may explain neuron loss in Parkinson's

    Excess α-synuclein killed cultured human dopamine neurons in a way that needed dopamine and reactive oxygen species, but protected non-dopamine cortical neurons. Soluble α-synuclein complexes with 14-3-3 protein were raised in the substantia nigra in Parkinson's disease.

    Xu et al. 2002 · Nat MedCultured human dopaminergic and cortical neurons, plus Parkinson's brain tissue
  21. 21
    Aging plus genestogether trigger ALS and frontotemporal dementia in mice

    TBK1, a major ALS and frontotemporal dementia gene, restrains the cell death enzyme RIPK1. Another RIPK1 brake, TAK1, falls in the aging human brain, and in mice with half the normal TBK1, lower TAK1 led to the hallmarks of ALS and frontotemporal dementia, which RIPK1 inhibition blocked.

    Xu et al. 2018 · CellMouse genetics plus aging human brain tissue
  22. 22
    Mitochondrial problemsin Down syndrome brain cells shift amyloid processing toward Aβ42

    Astrocytes and neurons from fetal Down syndrome brain processed the amyloid precursor protein abnormally, with less protective secreted APP and buildup of insoluble Aβ42 inside cells, a pattern copied by blocking mitochondria in normal astrocytes. Secreted APP markedly improved survival of Down syndrome neurons.

    Busciglio et al. 2002 · NeuronCultured fetal Down syndrome astrocytes and neurons, plus Down syndrome and Alzheimer's brain tissue
  23. 23
    REST lossin bipolar disorder brain organoids and patient cortex, reversed by lithium

    Brain organoids from people with bipolar disorder showed altered development, higher network activity and reduced nuclear REST. Lowering oxygen to physiological levels or treating with lithium restored REST, and reduced nuclear REST was also seen in the prefrontal cortex of patients with bipolar disorder.

    Meyer et al. 2024 · Mol PsychiatryHuman cerebral organoids from several iPSC lines, plus postmortem patient cortex
  24. 24
    Gliamay be a major source of amyloid-β in the brain

    Amyloid-β is normally made and secreted through the cell's secretory pathway by many cell types. Human astrocytes produced more amyloid-β than any other cell type tested.

    Busciglio et al. 1993 · Proc Natl Acad Sci U S ACultured neuronal and non-neuronal cells, including human astrocytes
  25. 25
    REST in the kidneyprotects filtering cells from injury during aging

    Mice lacking REST in podocytes, the kidney's filtering cells, developed protein in the urine, podocyte death and scarring during aging and were more vulnerable to kidney injury. REST rose in human podocytes with age, suggesting the same stress defense acts outside the brain.

    Magassa et al. 2021 · J Am Soc NephrolMouse knockouts, cultured podocytes, organ-on-chip and human kidney tissue
